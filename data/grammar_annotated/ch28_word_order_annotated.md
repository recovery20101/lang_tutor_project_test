# Chapter 28: Word order

Word order in Spanish is very **flexible** compared to English. Positioning of the subject in relation to the verb is governed largely by **emphasis and focus**, not rigid grammatical rules. Three word orders may express the same basic content, depending on where emphasis falls:

| Spanish | Focus |
|---|---|
| Asistió mucha gente a la fiesta. | The quantity of people — new info last |
| A la fiesta asistió mucha gente. | What happened at the party |
| Mucha gente asistió a la fiesta. | The standard neutral order |

The guidelines in this chapter should be regarded as **tendencies**, not unbreakable rules — except where stated otherwise.

## **28.1 New versus old information**

**Rule:** **New or important information** typically comes at or towards the **end** of a sentence in Spanish. 'Old' or already-established information comes earlier.

Three consequences follow from this principle.

### **28.1.1 Subject often follows the verb in answers to questions**

**Rule:** When answering a *¿quién?* or *¿qué?* question where the **subject** supplies the answer, the subject usually comes **after** the verb — because it is the new information.

**Examples:**

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| — ¿Quién fue a la cena? — Fueron Ana, Lole y Miguel. | 'Who went to the dinner?' 'Ana, Lole and Miguel went.' |
| — ¿Qué se ha caído? — Se ha caído el puente. | 'What has fallen down?' 'The bridge has fallen down.' |

### **28.1.2 Indefinite subjects often follow the verb**

**Rule:** A subject preceded by an indefinite article, numeral or indefinite adjective (*mucho*, *alguno* etc.) is usually the **main bearer of new information** and therefore tends to follow the verb. Exception: if the focus is on the **verb/verb phrase itself**, the indefinite subject comes first.

**Examples:**

| Spanish | English | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Llegaron un montón de policías. | A load of police showed up. | subject is new info — post-verbal |
| Faltaban tres chicas. | Three girls were missing. | subject is new info — post-verbal |
| Han desaparecido unos cuantos libros. | Several books have disappeared. | subject is new info — post-verbal |
| Mucha gente se fue. | Many people left. | focus on leaving — subject first |
| Se fue mucha gente. | Many people left. | focus on quantity — subject last |

### **28.1.3 Subject often follows when a direct object pronoun is present**

**Rule:** A direct object pronoun signals that its referent is **already known** (old information). In such cases the **subject** provides the newest piece of information and tends to come **after** the verb.

**Examples:**

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| — ¡Qué cuadro más bonito! — Lo compró Carlos. | 'What a beautiful picture!' 'Carlos bought it.' |
| — Me gusta tu pelo. — Me lo cortó Silvia. | 'I like your hair.' 'Silvia cut it for me.' |

## **28.2 Announcing a topic at the beginning of a sentence**

**Rule:** A very common Spanish strategy is to **introduce a topic** at the beginning of a sentence and then say something about it — equivalent to English 'As for Jones, he's emigrated to Australia'. This is achieved simply by placing the relevant words at the beginning. The topic is then typically **resumed** by a pronoun later in the sentence.

**Examples:**

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Las cañerías las instalo mañana. | I'll install the pipes tomorrow. |
| A Manolo lo he visto esta mañana. | I saw Manolo this morning. |
| En esta fábrica trabaja Andrea. | Andrea works in this factory. |
| Ayer vinieron los primos. | Yesterday the cousins came. |

## **28.3 Emphatic stress — fronting for emphasis**

**Rule:** Words and phrases that receive **unusually heavy emphatic stress** are often placed at the **beginning** of the sentence, even though they are not topics but bearers of important information.

**Examples:**

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Esos zapatos me quiero poner. | It's those shoes I want to wear. |
| A ese hombre han detenido. | That's the man who has been arrested. |

## **28.4 Specific constructions**

### **28.4.1 Subjects with no article must follow the verb**

**Rule:** When the subject is a **bare noun** (no article), it **must** be placed after the verb.

**Examples:**

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Entra luz por la persiana. | Light is coming in through the blind. |
| Viven lobos en aquellas montañas. | There are wolves in those mountains. |

### **28.4.2 Subjects of reflexive passive constructions**

**Rule:** In reflexive passive constructions, the subject typically comes **after** the verb, unless the subject constitutes the **topic** of the sentence (in which case it may come first).

**Examples:**

| Spanish | English | Subject position |
|---|---|---|
| No se veían las caras de los niños. | You couldn't see the faces of the children. | post-verbal (default) |
| No se comentó la gravedad de la situación. | The seriousness of the situation was not mentioned. | post-verbal (default) |
| Esos platos se ponen aquí. | Those plates go here. | pre-verbal (topic) |
| Una deuda tan grande no se liquida fácilmente. | Such a big debt can't be paid off easily. | pre-verbal (topic) |

### **28.4.3 Long subjects tend to follow the verb**

**Rule:** Long subject phrases — especially those containing a **relative clause** — are normally placed **after** the verb, particularly when the verb has no object.

**Examples:**

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Habló uno que es experto en antropología. | Someone who is an expert in anthropology spoke. |

### **28.4.4 Subject of a relative clause tends to follow the verb**

**Rule:** If a relative clause has an explicit subject, it tends to follow the verb — especially if placing it before would strand the verb at the very end of the clause.

**Examples:**

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| ¿Has visto la casa que se han comprado Pepi y Juan? | Have you seen the house that Pepi and Juan have bought? |
| He perdido el libro que me regaló Pablo. | I've lost the book that Pablo gave me. |

## **28.5 Position of adverbs**

### **28.5.1 Qualifying adverbs and adverbial phrases**

**Rule:** Qualifying adverbs are most common **immediately after the verb**. They can also follow the verb's object, or (for emphasis of the verb) precede the verb.

**Examples:**

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Ganaron fácilmente a los ingleses. | They easily beat the English. |
| Ganaron a los ingleses fácilmente. | They easily beat the English. |
| Fácilmente ganaron a los ingleses. | They easily beat the English. |

### **28.5.2 Adverbs of time and place**

**Rule:** Words like *aquí* 'here', *anoche* 'last night' come at the **end** or **beginning** of the sentence, depending on whether they carry **new information** (end — see 28.1) or constitute the **topic** (beginning — see 28.2).

### **28.5.3 Sentence adverbs**

**Rule:** Adverbs that modify the **entire sentence** (not just the verb) usually appear at the **beginning**. Critical note: unlike English, a Spanish adverb **cannot** be inserted between *haber* and a past participle.

**Examples:**

| Spanish | English |
|---|---|
| Afortunadamente dejaron de interesarse por el asunto. | Fortunately they lost interest in the matter. |
| Siempre lo he dicho. | I've always said this. (correct) |
| Lo he dicho siempre. | I've always said this. (correct) |
| ~~Lo he siempre dicho.~~ | (incorrect — adverb between *haber* and participle) |

## **28.6 Subject-verb inversion in questions**

### **28.6.1 General rules for subject-verb inversion**

**Rule:**
- In **direct questions** with an explicitly mentioned subject: inversion is **obligatory** for all questions except yes/no questions.
- In **indirect questions**: inversion is optional but preferred in most cases. It is **obligatory** in three specific contexts.

**Obligatory inversion in indirect questions:**
- **(a)** After *dónde* with verbs like *vivir*, *estar*
- **(b)** After *cuándo* with verbs of arrival/departure
- **(c)** After *qué* when functioning as direct object

**Examples:**

| Spanish | English | Type |
|---|---|---|
| ¿Dónde está el teatro? | Where is the theatre? | direct — obligatory inversion |
| ¿Mario sabe nadar? / ¿Sabe Mario nadar? | Can Mario swim? | yes/no direct — inversion optional |
| No sé cómo lo aguanta Beatriz. / No sé cómo Beatriz lo aguanta. | I don't know how Beatriz puts up with him. | indirect — optional |
| Pregúntale dónde vive Marisa. | Ask him where Marisa lives. | indirect — obligatory (dónde + vivir) |
| Hay que enterarse de cuándo sale el vuelo. | We need to find out when the flight departs. | indirect — obligatory (cuándo + departure) |
| No sé qué le dijo Nicolás. | I don't know what Nicolás said to him. | indirect — obligatory (qué as object) |

### **28.6.2 Subject-verb inversion with infinitives and gerunds**

**Rule:** When an infinitive or gerund is used with a finite verb in a question, the subject can be placed either **between the finite verb and the infinitive/gerund** (focus on verb) or **after the infinitive/gerund** (focus on subject).

**Examples:**

| Spanish | English | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| ¿Está Mercedes cantando? | Is Mercedes singing? | focus on action |
| ¿Está cantando Mercedes? | Is Mercedes singing? | focus on subject |
| ¿Qué tienen ellos que hacer? | What do they have to do? | focus on action |
| ¿Qué tienen que hacer ellos? | What do they have to do? | focus on subject |
