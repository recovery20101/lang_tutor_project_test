# Chapter 1 The alphabet, pronunciation, stress, spelling and punctuation

## **1.1 The alphabet** 

Due to recent changes, the composition of the Spanish alphabet today is more like English and other Romance languages. Because older reference works used a different system of classification, a few words of explanation may be helpful. 

Until 1994, **ch** and **ll** were considered to be separate letters in the Spanish alphabet. Consequently, in dictionaries and word lists there were separate listings for words beginning with each of them, after **c** and **l** respectively. Likewise, this alphabetical order was observed when those letters occurred within words, with the result that **cocha** would be listed after all other words beginning with **coc** -, and **callada** after **calzo** . In 1994, under international pressure and the impact of computer sorting programs, the Association of Academies of Spanish adopted the internationally accepted standard of alphabetical order, no longer considering **ch** and **ll** to be separate letters. However, as a distinctive feature of the language, Spanish still considers **ñ** to be a separate letter. Dictionaries, therefore, still retain a separate listing for the few words beginning with **ñ** (after those beginning with **n** ) and this order is preserved when **ñ** occurs within words ( **caña** being listed after **canzonetista** ).

## **1.2 Pronunciation** 

The only sure way of pronouncing Spanish correctly is to listen closely and try to imitate native speakers. However, a list of the Spanish letters together with their usual pronunciation is given in Table 1.1. This is offered as general guidance, drawing attention to instances where native speakers of English commonly make mistakes. Moreover, only the most important differences between Peninsular and Latin American Spanish are highlighted.

## **1.3 Stress and written accents** 

**Rule:** The correct pronunciation of Spanish depends not only on being able to reproduce the correct sound for each letter, but on applying the correct emphasis to each syllable in individual words. Incorrect stress may mean that listeners have difficulty in understanding your meaning, and in some cases may even change the meaning of words.

### **1.3.1 Syllables: basic principles**

**Rule:** For the purpose of identifying the syllables in Spanish words in order to understand stress and written accents, it is generally sufficient to know the following basic principles: 

- (a) Syllables should end in a vowel as far as possible: **ta-ba-co**, **po-pu-lar**. 
- (b) Combinations of consonants ending in **-l** or **-r**, as well as **ch**, cannot be split: **a-pli-car**, **re-gre-so**, **ca-lle**, **cu-cha-ra**. 
- (c) **s** does not belong to the same syllable as a following consonant: **cas-ta-ña**, **pos-tre**.

**Note:** For purposes of identification only, stressed vowels are underlined in the examples below.

#### 1.3.2.1 Stress on words ending in Vowel, N, or S

**Rule:** When words end in a single vowel or the consonants **n** or **s**, the stress normally falls on the next to last (penultimate) syllable.

**Examples:**
* v<u>e</u>rde
* much<u>a</u>cho
* n<u>o</u>ches
* v<u>e</u>nden
* vol<u>u</u>men

#### 1.3.2.2 Stress on words ending in other consonants

**Rule:** When words end in consonants other than **n** or **s**, the stress normally falls on the last syllable.

#### 1.3.2.3 Written acute accents (tilde)

**Rule:** When words are stressed in ways that do not conform to the above rules, the stress is indicated by a written acute accent (´). A missing accent can completely change the meaning of a word.

**Examples:**
* c<u>á</u>mara, fácil, Per<u>ú</u>
* **llego** (I arrive) vs. **llegó** (he/she arrived)

---

**Written acute accents: Specific Rules**

**Rules:**
1. A written accent always indicates the location of the stress.
2. Words stressed on syllables other than the last or next to last (e.g., *esdrújulas*) **always** require an accent: **enérgico**, **miércoles**.
3. **Plurals:** Stress normally remains on the same syllable in plural forms, which may cause an accent to appear or disappear: **joven** > **jóvenes**, **interés** > **intereses**.

### **1.3.4 Other uses of written acute accents (Diacritics)**

**Rule:** Written acute accents are used to distinguish between the meanings of words with the same spelling (homonyms).

**Examples:**

| Unaccented | Meaning | Accented | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **si** | if | **sí** | yes |
| **mi** | my | **mí** | me (obj. of prep.) |
| **tu** | your | **tú** | you (subject) |
| **el** | the | **él** | he / him |
| **se** | reflexive pronoun | **sé** | I know / be (imp.) |
| **de** | of | **dé** | give (subjunctive) |
| **te** | you (object) | **té** | tea |
| **aun** | even | **aún** | still / yet |
| **mas** | but | **más** | more |

### **1.4.2 Acronyms and abbreviations**

**Rule:** Capitals are used for acronyms. To indicate the plural of an abbreviation consisting of single letters, the letters are doubled.

**Rule:** Spanish often forms lowercase nouns and adjectives directly from acronyms to describe members of an organization.

**Examples:**

| Spanish | English | Note |
|---|---|---|
| la **CGT** | the CGT | Acronym |
| la **ONU** | UN | Acronym |
| las **CCAA** | the Autonomous Regions | Plural (Comunidades Autónomas) |
| las **FFAA** | the Armed Forces | Plural (Fuerzas Armadas) |
| **e**merretista | a member of the MRTA | Noun derived from acronym |

## **1.5 Punctuation**

### **1.5.1 Common Punctuation Marks**

#### 1.5.1.1 Numbers and Decimals (Regional Variation)

**Rule:** In most Spanish-speaking countries (except Mexico), the functions of the full stop and comma in numbers are the reverse of English.

**Examples:**

| Mark | Spanish Name | Use Case | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **.** | **Punto** | Thousands separator | **5.651.242** (5,651,242) |
| **,** | **Coma** | Decimal point | **21,6** (21.6) |

**Rule:** Spanish uses a colon (:) after salutations in letters, and unique inverted marks (¿, ¡) to open questions and exclamations.

#### 1.5.1.3 Quotes and Hyphens

**Rule:** Traditional Spanish uses angle quotes (« »), though modern usage often adopts English-style quotes. Hyphens are used less frequently than in English due to the tendency to form single compound words.

**Examples:**

| Mark | Spanish Name | Use Case | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **« »** | **Comillas** | Quotations | **«La paz»** |
| **-** | **Guión** | Compound words | **franco-alemán** |
| **–** | **Raya** | Reported speech | **—¿Cómo estás? —dijo.** |

**Note on compounds:** Many words that are hyphenated in English are single words in Spanish, e.g., **antirrobo** (anti-theft).

#### 1.5.1.4 Brackets and Other Marks

**Rule:** Parentheses and square brackets function similarly to their English counterparts.

**Examples:**
* **( ) Paréntesis** (parentheses)
* **[ ] Paréntesis cuadrado / rectangular** (square brackets)
* **. . . Puntos suspensivos** (dots/ellipsis)
* **; Punto y coma** (semicolon)
